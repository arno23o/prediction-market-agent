# A Betting Agent That Learns From Its Own Record

*Project brief — July 6, 2026*

*Written 2026-07-06; the design has changed since, see the README.*

## Page 1 — The idea

**The goal.** Build an agent that places bets on prediction markets — starting on Kalshi, the US-regulated exchange that welcomes automated traders — and gets better at it over time. The interesting part is how it improves. Most systems that learn from experience do it by retraining — adjusting the model itself, the way reinforcement learning does. Here the model stays fixed. Instead, the agent keeps a detailed written record of every attempt it has ever made, and each new attempt begins by studying that record. The core question behind the project: can a growing library of experience, read at the right moment, produce the same steady climb that retraining normally provides?

**How an attempt works.** Each attempt is a fresh, standalone run with two phases. First, thinking: scan the markets, pick a target, and explain why it looks beatable — what do we know, or what can we model, that the current price doesn't reflect? Nothing about where to look is handed down from above; finding the market's weak spot is part of each attempt's job. Second, building: create the model the idea calls for, turn it into probabilities, compare them to the market's prices, and bet where the gap is large enough.

**Play to the machine's strengths.** The building phase should be ambitious, because the builders are AI agents and serious modeling has become cheap for them. An agent can prototype a complex model in code, test it against history, find the flaw, and rebuild it a different way — several times before lunch. Work that would cost a human competitor a week costs an attempt an hour. So no attempt should settle for quick heuristics or rough reasoning out of any sense of limited capacity: if an idea deserves a full simulation, a blend of forecast models, or an algorithm tuned on years of data, that is what gets built. This speed of prototyping, testing, and pivoting is our structural advantage in these markets, and it may well be where the edge itself comes from.

**Two rules keep attempts honest.** Before any money moves, the attempt must write down its claimed edge — a short, specific argument for why these bets will make money — along with a prediction of what we should observe if the idea is right, and what we'd expect to see if it's wrong. Because this is written before the outcome is known, a lucky win can't be dressed up afterward as skill. And every bet must resolve within three days, so feedback arrives quickly.

We frame each attempt as an **edge claim** rather than a single wager. One claim can back many bets: if we believe we can forecast tomorrow's high temperature in New York better than the market can, the same idea works in ten cities, and ten outcomes tell us far more than one. Single bets are still allowed; they're just claims with a sample size of one.

**The ledger.** The backbone of the whole system is a permanent, searchable record of every attempt: the edge claim, the hypothesis, the reasoning, the data used, the bets placed, the outcomes, and — once the bets resolve — a retrospective on what went right or wrong. Every entry is tagged and summarized so future attempts can find what's relevant quickly. The ledger is the system's memory, and its quality determines whether anything else works.

**How it runs.** Two attempts per day to start, increasing if the results earn it, with no human in the approval path — the loop is autonomous inside its safety limits. Every bet is one dollar, which keeps attempts comparable and results easy to pool. Most bets are on paper, recorded at real market prices; a subset is placed with real money to verify that the pipeline truly works, from finding a market to getting paid. Every position is held until the market resolves — no trading out early — so each bet is a clean test of the probability it was built on.

**Shared, neutral tools.** Attempts share common plumbing — finding markets, reading prices, placing orders — so each run doesn't rebuild it from scratch. These tools carry no opinions and no advice. All judgment lives inside the attempts and the ledger, never in the infrastructure.

**Why it's worth doing.** Beyond the money, this is a clean testbed for a real research question: can an agent improve at an open-ended task purely through accumulated written experience? Markets keep objective score, which makes the answer measurable. Once the basic loop runs, it becomes a platform for experiments — different models, one loop versus two, memory on versus off. But the plan is to start simple: bare-bones infrastructure, one working loop, and a ledger worth trusting.

## Page 2 — Pushback, open questions, and next steps

**The central risk: luck looks exactly like skill.** Over three days and a handful of bets, a sound idea loses money often and a bad one wins often. If future attempts study the ledger with profit as the measure of what worked, the system will learn superstitions — imitating attempts that got lucky and abandoning ones that were right but unlucky. The pre-declared hypothesis is the best defense, so take it seriously at grading time: every resolved attempt should receive a verdict — right for the stated reason, right but not for the stated reason, or wrong — recorded separately from the money. And add one pooled scoreboard across all attempts: when we paid forty cents for something, did it happen more than forty percent of the time? Single attempts are too noisy to judge alone; the pool is where skill becomes visible. The flat one-dollar stakes keep those comparisons clean.

**Dollar bets verify the plumbing; they can't measure the edge.** With every stake at a dollar, be clear-eyed about what the real-money subset can and can't tell us. Kalshi charges a fee on every order, rounded up to the next cent, and the rounding hits small orders hardest: about four cents on a one-dollar stake, a four percent handicap before the bet even starts, with the gap between buying and selling prices on top. So the dollars won and lost on real bets will be mostly fees and noise — they exist to prove that orders fill and settle, and that's enough. The learning signal comes from the recorded prices and outcomes across all bets, paper and real alike; that's where the truth about the edge lives.

**The edge must be found, not assumed.** These markets are not sleepy — professionals and other bots are active in the popular ones — so any edge has to be genuinely discovered, and we deliberately aren't deciding in advance where it lives. Hunting is part of every attempt: look across what's on offer, reason about which prices look soft and why, and make the case. What each attempt brings to that hunt is the strength described on page one — the ability to build and iterate real models remarkably fast — which should let it test ideas that others would dismiss as too much work for markets this size. One piece of structure still helps: each attempt should tag its claim with the kind of edge it's asserting, in its own words. Over time the ledger will reveal, from the record rather than from anyone's prior beliefs, which kinds actually pay.

**"Learn from previous attempts" still needs one concrete decision: what does a new attempt actually read?** The archive will quickly outgrow what fits in front of an agent. I'd suggest three layers: a one-paragraph standard summary of every attempt, the full record of the few most relevant ones found through tags, and a short evolving playbook of distilled lessons, each citing the attempts behind it. Two dangers to design against. An attempt grading its own homework will write flattering retrospectives — have a separate, later pass do the grading. And early lessons drawn from a handful of noisy results can harden into false doctrine — playbook entries should carry the weight of evidence behind them and stay open to revision.

**Attempts can be independent, but not completely.** They share one set of markets, so two attempts could take opposite sides of the same bet or pile onto the same one. And while flat dollar stakes make any single bet harmless, a runaway attempt could still place hundreds of them. A thin layer above the attempts should own the safety rules: a cap on bets per attempt, a daily limit on real dollars with a kill switch, and a record of overlaps. One structural point follows from the three-day window: an attempt finishes before its bets resolve, so settling and writing the retrospective is a second pass that runs days later. The ledger should track each attempt through those stages — proposed, placed, resolved, reviewed.

**Where we've landed.** Decisions from the first round of discussion, now settled. We build on Kalshi alone for its ease of access in the US — it's regulated, its official API welcomes automated traders, and it has a practice environment; Polymarket can wait until its US product matures. We start at two attempts per day and increase only if the results earn it. Every bet is one dollar; most are paper, and a subset is placed for real purely to verify the pipeline. No human approves bets — the loop is fully autonomous inside its safety caps. And every position is held to resolution.

**Suggested next steps, in order.** First, open the Kalshi account, get API keys, and place one dollar bet end to end — practice environment first, then real. Second, design the ledger before anything else: its fields, its tags, its lifecycle. It's the backbone and deserves its own short spec. Third, write down the scoring rules — profit on stake, the pooled price-versus-outcome check, and hypothesis verdicts. Fourth, build the neutral tool layer. Fifth, start the loop: at two attempts per day, the first ten days produce about twenty attempts, and since the ledger starts empty, those runs double as the memory-free baseline that later learning is measured against. Success for this phase isn't profit — it's a loop that runs cleanly and a ledger that reads well. Profit is the phase after that.
